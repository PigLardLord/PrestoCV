# 0023. Ground the AI draft by quoting the description and explaining every word

- **Status:** Accepted
- **Date:** 2026-10-13
- **Amended by:** [ADR 0031](0031-let-the-ai-draft-write-around-the-facts.md) (2026-10-27): the summary and the role descriptions also keep the sentences that state no fact of their own, and a skill the description does not name may stay as an inferred one, without a level ((b) for those skills, (c) and (d) for the summary and the role descriptions). The rules of the facts stay as decided here.
- **Related:** [ADR 0022](0022-ship-legacy-app-as-v1.md), issues #16, #17, #18, #34 and #35

## Context

- **The decision that drives this.** Version 1 drafts a CV from the user's own description with Apple's on-device model. On 2026-10-13 the owner postponed the release so that the draft ships, and asked for test-driven development to stop regressions.
- **The model.** The on-device model is small. It paraphrases, leaves out jobs the user mentioned, and invents names, numbers and employers.
- **What nine review rounds showed.** They reviewed a sanitiser that recognises employers, places and names in free text. They kept finding the same two kinds of defect:
  - **invented data reaching the editor:** people, employers or numbers the user never wrote;
  - **facts moving between jobs:** one job's dates or results attributed to another job, especially when the model left that other job out.
- **Why patching did not work.** Each fix depended on capitalisation, prepositions or the model's own list of jobs, and each opened a new case. One round removed a regression test to get its change through.

## Decision

- **(a) The contract comes first, as tests.**
  - **The table.** `GroundingContractTests` is a data-driven table. Each row holds a description (in any of the six languages), the model's draft, and the draft expected after grounding.
  - **What it covers.** Every counterexample found in the review rounds is a row, and so are the positive controls: the app's example description in six languages, each with a faithful draft.
  - **Rows are never removed.** A row is never removed or weakened. A new finding adds a row, which fails, before any fix.
- **(b) Facts are quotes.** A structured value is kept only if it occurs verbatim in the description. This covers:
  - name, email, phone and link;
  - title, employer, school, degree, location;
  - dates, certification, skill, language, interest, figure.
  - **Amended by [ADR 0031](0031-let-the-ai-draft-write-around-the-facts.md):** a skill the description does not name may stay as an inferred technology or soft skill, without a level. A skill the description names is still a quote.

  Case, diacritics and white space are ignored. Digits are folded only for numbers, never inside addresses. Addresses are compared as whole tokens. Each kept value records where it occurs in the description.
  - **Dates the model writes in another form.** If the model writes a date in another form ("2016-01-01"), the value is cut to what the description wrote (the year "2016"). If that part is not a quote either, the date is dropped.
  - **The literal "null"** stands for an empty field.
  - **The person's own fields** (name, contacts, location, title) need no backing clause, because they describe the person, not a job. They must still be quoted from a clause that speaks for the person (c) or from a verbless clause, and the headline title and the place from one that claims them (c). So "My sister's email is anna@example.com" gives no email, and "I would like to work as a nurse in Turin" gives neither the title nor the place. A contact (email, phone, link) is the person's only in three shapes:
    - a verbless clause ("Email: me@example.com");
    - the person's possession of the field ("My email is me@example.com");
    - a verb of contacting whose object is the person ("You can reach me at…", "Contact me at…", "Contattami a…"), or the person as the subject of being reachable, from a short closed list per language ("I can be reached at…", "Sono raggiungibile a…", "Ich bin erreichbar unter…", "Je suis joignable à…").

    In each shape, the clause names no other person. Beyond the shape's own words (the label's field; the possessive with its field and "to be"; the reader's lead-in, such as "You can", "Feel free to" or "Non esitate a", with the verb of contacting and the person as its object), each word of the clause is a contact or field word, a number or the contact itself, a function or time word, or one of the person's own quotes (name, place, title). A name or any other noun gives no contact: "Reference: Anna Verdi, anna@example.com" and "Puoi contattarmi tramite Anna a anna@example.com" give no email. Any other clause gives no contact, even one the person speaks. So "I can put you in touch with my manager at boss@example.com" and "I can put you in touch with the recruiter at rec@example.com" give no email.
    - **References.** A pasted CV often ends with its references, names and addresses on separate lines. From the first sentence that holds a reference word, from a short closed list per language (reference, referee, recommendation; referente, referenza, raccomandazione; Referenz, Referenzperson, Empfehlung; referencia, recomendación; référence, recommandation; referência, recomendação), to the end of the description, no contact is the person's, in any shape. So "Email: me@example.com. References available on request." keeps the email, and "References. Anna Verdi, manager at Acme. anna@acme.com" gives none.
    - **A verbless contact** (a label, or an address on its own) is the person's only when the sentence before it, if there is one, names no other person: none of its clauses speaks for someone else, names another person or holds a reference word. And every verbless clause it leans on that stands alone, a sentence with no verb ("Anna Verdi.") or a verbless clause at the start of the contact's own sentence that no clause with a verb precedes ("Anna Verdi; Email: …"), is explained: contact or field words, numbers, function or time words, or the person's own quotes (name, place, title). An unexplained word, a name above all, gives no contact: "Anna Verdi. Email: anna@example.com" and "I'm Maria Rossi. Anna Verdi; Email: anna@example.com" give no email. A verbless fragment that follows a clause with a verb in its sentence belongs to that clause (c), so "I'm Anna Verdi, a nurse in Turin. Email: …" keeps its contacts. In such a fragment, a capitalised word that is not the first word of its sentence is a quote of the draft that the description backs (the person's name, place or title, an employer, a school…) or a contact or field word; otherwise the sentence names someone else, and no verbless contact leans on it: "I'm Maria Rossi, a friend of Anna Verdi. Email: anna@example.com" gives no email. So "Reference: Anna Verdi. Email: anna@example.com" and "Anna is a nurse. Email: anna@example.com" give no email, and neither does "My sister is a nurse. Email: me@example.com": a loss, not a leak (f).
  - **Another entry's quoted words.** A job's title, or a degree, is dropped when it is quoted only in a clause that backs another entry.
    A clause that names no entry, and whose words are all explained by the closed vocabulary, the entry's own quotes or the person's own quotes, backs no other entry: a title quoted there stays, unless the job has ended (next item).
  - **The headline is not the title of a job that has ended.** A clause that names no entry says what the person is now ("I am a nurse in Leeds"). For a job that the description says is over, it states no title of that job, so the job's title stands only if the clause that names the job gives it ("I was a forklift driver at Kestrel Foods"); otherwise the field stays empty and the employer, the dates and the rest stay. Owner decision of 2026-10-26: copying the current profession onto a past job, when the description does not state the role there, is an invention, not a harmless gap-filler. A job is over when no clause of it says that it goes on (a word for the present, or "since" in a sentence with one year), and some clause of it, with the clauses of nothing but time next to it, writes two different years ("from 2014 to 2018") or an end word ("until", "formerly", "bis", "hasta", "jusqu'à", "fino a"), and no word for the present. The clauses of a job are those that back it, or, when none does, those that state it as a fact, with the clauses of nothing but time next to them that back no entry: "Antes, de 2015 a 2019, trabalhei na agência Pixel Gaúcho" says the job is over although "agência" leaves its clause unexplained (#354). A job that goes on and a degree keep the title that the headline gives. The limits are known, and each fails by keeping the title, never by dropping it: a job that no clause states as a fact, a job in the past tense with no dates, and a Portuguese "até 2018" with a single year (the word is also "even") are not found over; a hedge does not make a job go on. The test-only counter of invented facts reads the same two-year rule on its own, from the sentences of the description, and flags the title as invented without using the production rule.
  - **The name of an employer or a school.** The words of the name of an employer or a school ground nothing but the name. A title, a degree or a place that the description gives only inside such a name is dropped: "contabile" in "Studio Contabile Merlini" is not the job of the person, nor "Vale Verde" in "Centro de Saúde de Vale Verde" the place of the job. The names are those the draft gives for its entries. The same word beside the name ("I worked as a nurse at Greenway Nurse Care") or in a clause of its own stays. A name that is the very text of the field is the same case: the title "Barista" of a job at "Barista", the place "Bristol" of a job at "Bristol", the degree "Cookery" of a study at "Cookery". The text then stands for the field only if the clause that backs the entry writes it once more than the name takes ("I worked as a Barista at Barista", "I worked at Bristol in Bristol"). This holds for the name of the entry's own employer or school only: "Siemens" stays the issuer of a certification, and "Lincoln" the place of a job, when it is the name of another entry. The limits fail by dropping, never by inventing: a clause that a comma splits ("I worked at Bristol, in Bristol") leaves the second text in a clause that does not back the job, and a field written once that equals the name of its own employer or school is dropped.
  - **A degree names a qualification.** A degree that is nothing but finite verbs, as the clause that quotes it reads them, is dropped, and the school stays: "terminé" in "Estudié en la Escuela de Arte Turia y terminé en 2017" is what the person did, not what they hold. A verb beside a noun ("Titulado en Magisterio") and a word that the vocabulary lists as a form of a verb but the clause uses as a noun ("Lehre", "curso", "nurse" in "I trained as a nurse") stay.
- **(c) A job's facts need a clause where every word is explained.**
  - **Clauses.** The description is split into clauses, at sentence ends, semicolons, commas, and the coordinating conjunctions of the six languages.
  - **Which facts.** A job's dates, its "current", its figures, its achievements and its location count only when they come from a clause that backs the job.
  - **When a clause backs a job.** The clause must name the job's employer, or its title when the job has no employer. And every word in it must be explained: it either belongs to the **closed vocabulary**, or lies inside one of that job's own quoted values. A job with a title and no employer and no date is none (a title alone is the person's own), but when the person states that title as what they are, and the draft gives no headline, the title is the headline (#354): a clause that claims something for the person says it after a lead-in that ends in the present of "to be" in the first person ("am", "sono", "bin", "soy", "suis", "sou") and at most an indefinite article, with nothing after it in its sentence but "and" and a clause that opens with the first-person pronoun ("I am a plumber", "Sono uno sviluppatore iOS", "Ich bin Bäckerin", "Soy camarero", "Je suis comptable", "Sou motorista de caminhão"). "I like long walks", "I want to be a nurse", "My mother is a nurse", "I am not a nurse", "I'm looking for a job as a nurse" and "I am a nurse assistant" state no "nurse". A title the person had is no headline either: "I was a nurse", "J'étais étudiant en 2015", "Ich bin Bäckerin gewesen" (the past, or the German perfect, of "to be"). A word after the title can make it no role the person holds ("Je suis médecin dans un jeu vidéo", "I am a nurse, but only in a game"), so "I am a nurse in Leeds" and "I am a nurse and only in a game" give no headline either: the safe way to fail. No list tells a verb from such a qualifier in Italian, Spanish and Portuguese, which drop the pronoun, so "Sono uno sviluppatore iOS e scrivo" gives no headline, and "Sono uno sviluppatore iOS e io scrivo" does. What follows the title is read in the sentence as written, not as the claimed clauses leave it: they leave out the denial of "I am a nurse, but not in real life", and the title would end its sentence there.
  - **The closed vocabulary.** These are short lists, written by hand and kept in the code, one per language: function words, CV verbs, time and level words, and the few words of studying and graduating ("degree", "Abschluss", "diplôme", "laurea"…).
  - **Clauses that join a backing clause.** A clause that names no employer still backs job E when two conditions hold. It sits in the same sentence as, and next to, a clause that backs E. And every one of its words is explained by the closed vocabulary or by E's own quotes. So:
    - "from 2012 to 2016," before the employer's clause keeps its years;
    - ", and trained 3 new teachers" after it keeps its achievement, when that achievement is quoted;
    - a neighbour clause with an unexplained word joins nothing.
  - **A joining clause** can be explained only by two things:
    - the closed vocabulary;
    - one of E's achievement quotes that lies inside that clause and contains none of the six languages' employer prepositions ("at", "for", "with", "da", "presso", "per", "bei", "für", "en", "para", "chez", "pour", "em", "na", "no" and the like).
      - **The description's language decides.** When that language is known, a preposition of another language that is an ordinary word of the description's language does not count as an employer preposition. Examples: French "ai" in "j'ai formé", English "per" in "12 patients per shift". A preposition that the description's language does not have still counts, so "trained 3 cooks chez Gamma" in an English description explains nothing. When the language is unknown, the union applies.
      - **The same reading for negations and hedges.** A negation or hedge of another language that is an ordinary word of the description's language is read as that ordinary word. Examples: German "Mai" (a month, not Italian "never"), English "fast" (not German "almost"), Portuguese "no" (not English "no").

    E's location, description, title or other quotes never explain a joining clause. So a draft that copies another employer's name into one of E's values cannot pull that employer's clause in.
  - **What a joining clause can give.**
    - **Dates and "current"** come from a joining clause only when it is a pure time phrase: years, and words of a short time-phrase list per language. That list holds the prepositions and adverbs of time and the conjunctions that join them ("from", "to", "since", "until", "and", "currently"; "dal", "al", "fino"; "seit", "bis", "von"…). The rest of the closed vocabulary does not count, because a word of it can be a name. So "from 2012 to 2016" and "since 2016" are time phrases, and "and at Next in 2020" is not: "at" and "next" are not in the list.
    - **A graduation phrase** is a pure time phrase that also holds words of graduating ("graduated", "laureata", "laureato", "Abschluss", "abgeschlossen", "diplômé", "obtenu", "graduado", "graduada", "formei", "formado"…). It joins only an education entry, and gives that entry its end date: "…and graduated in 2012" gives the degree 2012. Next to a job's clause it joins nothing, so a job never takes a graduation year. An achievement quote that holds a word of graduating never explains a job's clause either.
    - **An achievement.** A joining clause explained by an achievement quote gives that achievement alone, never a date, "current" or another figure. The quote must begin with a CV verb of the closed vocabulary ("trained", "ho formato", "formé"…). So "…and Beta employed me in 2019" joins nothing.
  - **A clause next to backing clauses of two different entries** joins neither.
  - **A completion year is never a start date.** A year that a clause writes with a word of graduating or finishing a course ("abgeschlossen", "completed", "conseguito", "terminé", "terminado", "concluí" and the like in the six languages) is the year the study ended, whichever clause writes it: for an education entry such a clause counts as a graduation clause even when it also names the school ("Ich habe 2016 meine Ausbildung an der Fachschule … abgeschlossen"). The end date may take its year; the start date never does, and stays empty when the description writes no other year for it. The limit is known and fails by dropping, never by inventing: a clause that writes a start year and a completion year together gives the start date nothing. A year written with a start ("since 2014", "seit 2014") stays the start date.
  - **One year, one date.** A year that occurs once backs one date only. When the model puts it in both dates of an entry, a job keeps it as its start date and an education or certification as its end date.
  - **Education and certifications** follow the same rule as jobs, with the school or the certification in the employer's role.
  - **Any other word makes the clause back nothing.** That covers another employer, in any letter case or left out of the draft; a longer name ("Acme Labs" for "Acme"); and a person.
  - **Clauses that deny, doubt or only hope back nothing.** The closed vocabulary holds no word of:
    - negation ("not", "never"…);
    - doubt or degree ("barely", "almost", "nearly", "partly"…);
    - wish or intention ("want", "would like", "hope", "plan"…);
    - future ("will", "werde"…);
    - condition ("if", "se", "wenn", "si"…).

    It lists the CV verbs only in their present and past forms. A CV verb in another tense or mood ("lavorerò", "trabajaría", "travaillerais") is a word the vocabulary does not explain. So such a clause backs and joins nothing.

    In an English description, "plan", "plans" and "planning" are words of intention only as verbs: with "to", "on", an article or a possessive after them, or a subject, a form of "to be", an auxiliary or a possessive before them ("I am planning to learn Rust", "I'm also planning a move", "My plan is to learn Spanish"). After a content word, an article or a preposition, or with nothing before them, they name an activity and are no such word: "route planning", "care plans", "responsible for planning", "Skills: planning". "I plan the night shifts", with "plan" between "I" and "the" and no preposition after it, is the verb of an activity (#354); "I plan the move to Berlin" and "I plan routes" keep the intention. So "Since 2018 I have worked in route planning at Acme" gives the Acme entry, and "I am good at SAP, route planning and team leadership" claims the three skills. "Since 2018 I have done route planning at Acme" still gives none: "done" is no CV verb, and "route planning" stands unexplained before the name. In a description of no known language the three words keep their intention. German "Plan" and "Pläne" are nouns in the same way, after a content word or an article ("Ich habe einen Plan erstellt"), and the person's intention after a possessive ("Mein Plan ist, Rust zu lernen"). The other languages name the activity with a noun of its own ("pianificazione", "Routenplanung", "planificación", "planification", "planejamento").
  - **An entry needs a factual mention.** An entry is kept only if some clause of the description names its employer, school or certification (or its title, when it has none of these), holds none of the words above, and has only factual verbs.
    - **A factual verb** is a CV verb in a present or past form that the vocabulary lists, or an auxiliary followed in the clause by such a form. The present and past of "to be" count ("am", "'m", "was"; "sono", "ero"; "bin", "war"; "soy", "era"; "suis", "étais"; "sou", "era"), so "I'm a nurse at Acme" is factual. The on-device lexical class tells which words are verbs, and contractions count as their full form.
      - **What grounding relies on.** Grounding reads the lexical class only in English and German, the languages whose rows hold with it, because a device tags a language only once it has downloaded its data. So Italian, Spanish, French and Portuguese are grounded the same on every device, and English and German only on a device that has their data: without it, grounding would take a wish, a plan or another person's statement for the person's fact. So on such a device it gives no draft, and says which language data the device needs (`languageDataUnavailable`):
        - the use case asks before the model writes a draft, and grounding asks again before it reads one;
        - the app then asks the system to download the data, and waits for its answer while the generating screen stays up, at most 60 seconds;
        - once the data is there, the app generates the draft once more, without another tap. When the system cannot give it, does not answer within the 60 seconds, or the device still lacks it after the second try, the app ends with a message that names what the person can do instead: write the CV by hand, import it, or describe themselves in Italian, Spanish, French or Portuguese. The message promises no time;
        - the system downloads the data, and the app sends nothing.
    - **Every word before the name must be explained.** Every word that leads in to the mention must be explained: by the closed vocabulary, by a number, or by the entry's or the person's own quotes. The lead-in is the words before the name, or before the clause's first factual verb, whichever comes first. The device does not tag the verbs of every language, so an unexplained word there may be a verb it cannot see. So "Cerco lavoro come infermiera", "I'm looking for a job as a nurse", "Sogno Acme dal 2027" and "Je rêve d'Acme" give no entry.
    - **Every verb before the name must be factual.** Any verb that comes before the name, or before the clause's first factual verb, and is not factual makes the mention not factual. That covers:
      - another verb in any form ("resterò", "try", "going");
      - an infinitive ("lavorare", "trabajar");
      - a verb after an infinitive marker ("to work", "zu arbeiten").
    - **A purpose or detail after the statement does not.** A verb that comes after both the name and a factual verb does not undo the mention. So "I worked at Acme to support 3 teams" and "Ho lavorato presso Acme per supportare 3 team" are factual, while "I studied hard to work at Acme" and "bei Acme zu arbeiten" are not. The words from the first such verb to the end of the clause give no facts.
    - **The person is the subject.** A clause speaks for the person only when its subject is the person. That means a first-person pronoun ("I", "io", "ich", "je", "yo", "eu"), or no subject at all in the way a CV writes ("Worked at Acme", "Ho lavorato presso Acme", a verbless clause). In the pro-drop languages a listed verb form must be first person singular ("ho lavorato", "lavoro"; not "ha lavorato", "lavora").
      - **The explicit subject.** It is the noun phrase before the clause's first verb. In German, when a phrase of time or place comes first, it is the phrase right after the verb. Only a first-person pronoun counts as the person. A noun ("a nurse"), a name ("Alice"), a third-person pronoun or a possession ("my sister") never does, even when the draft quotes the same word.
        - **Two exceptions** are about the person:
          - the person's possession of one of their own fields ("my email", "my phone", "my profile", "my page", "my English", "my skills"; "la mia email", "il mio inglese"; "meine E-Mail"…), from a short closed list per language of field words;
          - a clause addressed to the reader whose object is the person ("You can reach me at…", "Puoi contattarmi a…").
        - **"My sister" stays another subject.** A possession of a person ("my sister", "my colleague") is never one of these.
        - **Languages that keep their subject.** English and French do not drop the subject. A clause whose first verb comes after anything but a pure time phrase needs a first-person pronoun before that verb. Otherwise the words before the verb are another subject. So "In Rome Anna speaks French (C2)" and "At Acme Anna worked" give nothing, while "In Rome I speak French" and "Since 2016 worked at Acme" keep their facts.
        - **German.** German reads its subject right after the second-position verb ("In Berlin arbeitete Anna bei Acme" gives nothing), because its CV clauses often end with the verb ("Bei Acme gearbeitet").
      - **Another subject.** A clause with another subject backs nothing, gives no mention and is never quoted. So "A nurse worked at Acme in 2019", "Alice managed a school" and "My colleague managed a school" give nothing. Third-person pronouns are not words of the closed vocabulary.
      - **A shared subject.** A clause that starts with its verb after a conjunction or a comma shares the subject of the clause before it. So "My sister is a nurse and worked at Acme" gives no Acme entry and no summary "Worked at Acme".
      - **Every fact is the person's.** Languages and their levels, skills, interests and certifications also need a clause that speaks for the person, or a verbless list ("Languages: Italian (native), French (C2)"). So "My sister speaks French (C2)" gives no French. A language may stay without a level when the description gives none.
      - **A claim, not a denial, a wish or a supposition.** Skills, interests, languages and the person's headline title and place count only in a clause of the person that claims them. The name and the contacts keep their own rules (b).
        - **What claims nothing.** A clause that holds a word of the modality lists claims nothing: negation, doubt, wish, future or condition. So does a clause that follows, in its sentence, a clause that opens with a word of condition ("if", "se", "wenn", "si"), and a clause that depends on such a clause: split off by a comma or a conjunction, with no verb, starting with its verb, or saying nothing as a fact ("Ich träume davon, Spanisch zu lernen"). A clause that states nothing as a fact (no verb, or none factual) and takes no fact from a clause before it shares the withdrawal of the clause after it in its sentence, too: "Spanisch zu lernen, davon träume ich", "Spanish, that's what I'd like to learn" and "Learning German and Spanish is what I'd like" give no language. So "I have never used Python", "I would like to learn Spanish", "If I had time, I would play chess" and "Si j'avais le temps, je jouerais aux échecs" give no skill, language or interest, and "I would like to work as a nurse in Turin" no title and no place. The same item in a plain clause of the person stays.
        - **Questions.** A sentence that ends with a question mark claims nothing, any clause of it: "Do I speak French?" and "Parlo francese e tedesco, giusto?" give no language. The sentence after it claims as usual: "Do you need a translator? I speak French." keeps French.
        - **Quotations.** The words inside quotation marks claim nothing, another person's words or the person's own quote: «…», »…«, ‹…›, “…”, „…“, „…”, ‚…‘, ‘…’, "…" and '…', over two sentences too. A mark opens only after a space, a bracket, a dash, a colon or another mark, and closes only before a space, a punctuation mark or the end, so an apostrophe inside a word ("I’m", "l’inglese") is no mark; a mark that nothing may close quotes nothing. A quote may hold a quote of another pair, a German „…“ one of “…” or ‚…‘ and a “…” one of „…“, and a closing mark closes the latest open quote that it pairs with, whichever pair that is: the “ that closes an inner „…“ is no end of the “…” that holds it, so the sentence after the inner quote is still quoted ("Mein Kollege schrieb “", "In meiner Tabelle ist das Wiederholungszeichen:", "”", "Mein Motto ist „Sauberer Code“. Ich benutze Swift.", "Ende meiner Nachricht.”" give no Swift). A mark that an inner quote took never ends the quote that holds it: when no other mark closes that quote, it runs to the end of the line of the last mark that may close it, as only a line break tells where another person's words end, so "Mein Kollege schrieb “Er sagte „Hallo”. Ich benutze Swift." gives no Swift. An inner quote that starts with a punctuation mark is not told apart: its opening mark, right before that mark, reads as a closing mark of the outer pair and ends it. A mark at the end of a line opens a quote that goes on with the next lines: "Mon collègue a écrit '", then "Je parle français.'" on the next line, gives no French, though no colon introduces the speech. A mark alone on its line closes the quote, so the line after it claims as usual: "My colleague wrote "", "I use Swift.", """, "I speak French." on four lines keep French. It does not when a later mark closes the quote for sure and none of the quotes opened after the lone one takes that later mark: the lone mark is then a symbol the quote holds, such as a ditto mark, and the quote runs to the later mark, so "My colleague wrote "", "In my table, the ditto symbol is:", """, "I speak French." and "End of my message."" on five lines give no French. A single mark right after a letter or a digit may end a word instead, whatever follows it: a possessive ("patients' records", "Hans’ Team"), an elision ("un po'", "jusqu' à") or a prime ("a 5' de la oficina"). It closes no quote for sure, so "My colleague said 'I manage patients' records. I speak French.'" gives no French. A quote that only such a mark may close runs to the end of the line of the last one, as only a line break tells where another person's words end: "My colleague said 'I manage patients' records. I speak French." gives no French either, and the next line claims as usual. An apostrophe outside a quote claims as usual ("I'm", "l'inglese", "dell'università", "my clients' apps"). The words outside the marks claim as usual: "I use “clean code” principles with Swift every day" keeps Swift.
        - **Another person's line.** A line that ends with a colon and does not speak for the person ("My colleague wrote:", "Il mio collega ha scritto:") introduces someone else's words: the line after it claims nothing, every sentence of it. The line after that claims as usual, and a colon line the person speaks introduces the person's own ("My languages:", "Skills:", "I worked with my team on:").
        - **Another subject.** A relative pronoun or a subordinator that brings in another subject withdraws what follows it in its clause, and the clauses that depend on that part: "who", "whom", "whose", "which", "that"; "che", "cui"; "der", "die", "das" and their forms, "welche"; "que", "quien", "cuyo"; "qui", "que", "dont"; "que", "quem", "cujo". So "I work with a colleague who speaks French", "Io lavoro con un collega che parla francese" and "Ich leite ein Team, das Kotlin und Swift nutzt" give no French, Kotlin or Swift. The person stays the subject when a pronoun of the first person follows ("which I use", "das ich nutze", "que je parle"), or, in the languages that drop the pronoun, a listed form of a verb that only the person says ("che uso", "que hablo", "que falo"). Spanish and Portuguese "que" brings in no subject right after a form of "tener" or "ter" that the person can say ("tengo", "tenía", "tuve", "he tenido", "había tenido"; "tenho", "tinha", "tive", "tenho tido", "tinha tido"): it makes their periphrasis of obligation, whose infinitive is the person's ("tengo que hablar", "tenho que falar"). A word that only ends the way an infinitive does keeps no person, as it is often a finite form of the other subject: "Je travaille avec une collègue qui prépare des cours de Python", "qui assure", "qui offre", "qui découvre", "che appare", "che compare", "que requer" and "quem souber" give no skill. German "der", "die" and "das" are articles too, and count only at the start of a clause that a comma or a conjunction split off; English "that" is a demonstrative too, and counts only before a verb, a pronoun of the third person, a possessive or an article ("in that role" keeps its item). An item before the pronoun stays: "I work with Python, which I know well" keeps Python.
        - **Moods of supposition.** A mood that only supposes or wishes is never a fact.
          - **German Konjunktiv II** ("würde", "hätte", "wäre", "müsste", "dürfte" and their forms) is in the modality list with its umlaut, and read unfolded, so the past "wurde", "hatte", "musste" and "durfte" stay facts: "Am liebsten würde ich Spanisch lernen" and "Ich würde als Lehrerin in Berlin arbeiten" give nothing, "Ich wurde befördert" claims.
          - **English "'d"** after a pronoun is "would" before a verb's base form or any other word ("I'd like", "I'd work", "I'd be"), and "had" before "been", "had" or a past form of the English verb lists, one that has no form in "-s" there ("I'd worked", "I'd used", "I'd taught").
          - **The conditionals** of "to be", "to have", "to do" and of the verbs of liking are in the modality lists: "sarei", "avrei", "farei", "vorrei", "piacerebbe"; "sería" (with its accent; the Portuguese "seria" also withdraws the Spanish typed without it), "tendría", "haría", "quisiera", "querría", "gustaría", "encantaría"; "serais", "aurais", "ferais", "voudrais", "aimerais", "souhaiterais", "adorerais"; "seria", "teria", "faria", "gostaria", "queria", "quereria", "adoraria".
        - **Wishes in a phrase.** A wish that only a phrase says is in the modality lists too, as its two words: "würde gern", "würde gerne", "würde lieber"; and the verbs of dream with their complement, "sogno di", "lo sogno", "sueño con", "lo sueño", "rêve de", "rêve d'", "en rêve", "sonho em", "sonho com", "träume davon", "träume von"; and the nouns of intention with their preposition, "intenzione di", "intención de", "intention de", "intention d'", "intenção de", "intention of", "intention to". The second word may stand anywhere after the first in the clause; the German phrases may stand in either order, as German fronts them ("Gerne würde ich Spanisch lernen", "Davon träume ich"). The other languages' phrases keep their order, which they do not front. The words alone keep their meaning: "I'd used Swift", "Ich wurde befördert" and the noun ("un sogno", "mein Traum", "l'intenzione del cliente") claim as before. German "plane" is the verb of intention beside "ich" ("Ich plane, Rust zu lernen", "Nächstes Jahr plane ich"), and "vorhaben" is read when its "vor" ends a clause that holds a form of "haben" ("Ich habe vor, Rust zu lernen", "Rust zu lernen, das habe ich vor"); "Ich habe vor 5 Jahren bei Acme gearbeitet" says a time, and keeps its entry.
        - **Ability and part are claims.** The forms of "can" ("posso", "kann", "puedo", "peux", "pode") and the words of part or most ("mostly", "partly", "teilweise", "parcialmente", "partiellement") qualify a claim without withdrawing it: "I can speak French" and "I partly use Swift" keep French and Swift. Both short lists are drawn from the modality lists.
        - **A negation that governs a high level denies the level.** When every such word of the clause is a negation or a hedge, and each one governs a high level, the clause still claims the item, without the level: "I am not an expert in Swift" keeps Swift. Denying a high level implies some knowledge. Denying a low level, or knowledge itself, denies the item: "I have no basic knowledge of German" and "Ich habe keine Grundkenntnisse in Deutsch" give no German. The heights come from the level vocabularies of the sanitiser: a beginner's skill and an A1 or A2 language are low ("basic", "beginner", "elementary", "Grundkenntnisse", "notions"), every other level is high ("intermediate", "advanced", "fluent", "expert", "native", B1 to C2). A negation governs a level when only forms of "to be", indefinite articles and other negations stand between them: "not an expert", "never been fluent", "non sono esperto", "kein Experte", "no soy experto", "ne suis pas expert", "não sou especialista". A negation of the ability itself withdraws the claim, though a word of level stands later in the clause: "I cannot speak German even at a basic level" and "I have never used Python at any level" give nothing.
    - **A clause without any verb** is factual when it starts a sentence or follows a semicolon ("Nurse at Acme, 2016–2020"; "Head nurse at Beta"). A verbless clause split off by a comma or a conjunction belongs to the clause before it in its sentence, and is factual only if that clause is. So "I have never worked at Acme or Beta" and "I would like to work at Acme, Beta or Gamma" give no Beta and no Gamma, while "I worked at Acme, Beta and Gamma" keeps all three.
    - **Backing.** The same rules decide whether a clause can back an entry.
    - So "I have never worked at Acme", "I would like to work at Acme", "Vorrei lavorare presso Acme", "Resterò presso Acme dal 2027", "I am going to work at Acme" and "Ho intenzione di lavorare presso Acme" give no Acme entry and no date.
    - The rule is about the form of the verbs, not a list of phrases. A future, a wish or a plan that the lists do not name still has a verb that is not a listed present or past form.
  - **What nothing depends on.** Capitalisation, prepositions and the model's list of jobs play no part.
- **(d) Prose is quoted from the description.**
  - **A prose clause is a quote.** Each clause of a summary sentence, a role description or an achievement must quote one clause of the description: the same words, in the same forms and the same order. Case, diacritics, white space and punctuation are ignored.
  - **The quote runs to the end of that clause**, or to the start of its purpose tail (c).
    - **The lead-in.** It may leave out only a lead-in: first-person pronouns and joining words ("I", "and", "where I"; "io", "e", "dove"; "ich", "und", "wo ich"; "je", "j'", "et", "où"; "yo", "y", "donde"; "eu", "e", "onde"), with the auxiliary or the form of "to be" that follows them ("I have", "I'm", "où j'ai", "ho", "sono", "ich habe").
    - **What it never leaves out.** An article, a word of negation, doubt, wish, future or condition, and any other word.
    - So "trained 3 new teachers" quotes "…, where I trained 3 new teachers", "taught at Acme since 2016" quotes "I have taught at Acme since 2016", and "A nurse in Turin" quotes "I'm a nurse in Turin".
  - **Why quotes.** Prose rebuilt from the description's words, even clause by clause and in order, reversed claims in review:
    - "I was trained by Acme" became "I trained Acme";
    - "My colleague managed a school" became "I managed a school";
    - a denial or a doubt was dropped.

    A quote keeps the user's own claim, with its subject, its voice and its negation.
  - **Summary.** A summary sentence holds no number, and each of its clauses quotes a clause of the description.
  - **Role descriptions and achievements** quote clauses that back their own job, outside any purpose tail (c).
  - **Amended by [ADR 0031](0031-let-the-ai-draft-write-around-the-facts.md):** a sentence of the summary or of a role description that quotes nothing also stays when it states no fact of its own (`WrittenText`). Achievements are still quotes, and a summary still holds no number.
  - So a paraphrase, an invented name ("Jane Smith", "Globex"), a change of person or a swapped number drops its sentence.
- **(e) Words are compared in a normalised form.**
  - That form is lower case and without diacritics. No lemma is used: a prose quote keeps the user's forms (d), and the closed vocabulary explains a CV verb only in the forms it lists (c), so neither a paraphrase nor a future becomes a fact.
- **(f) Losing beats inventing.** A dropped fact is acceptable: the editor's banner asks the user to complete the draft. An invented or misattributed fact blocks the release.
- **(g) Tidying after grounding only removes or reorders.** These steps add nothing:
  - **A skill that equals an interest of the same draft, or is part of one, is dropped when the person mentions it only as leisure.** A hobby is not a claimed ability, and the interest stays. "cozinhar para os amigos" of the interest "cozinhar para os amigos ao fim de semana" is one (#354).
  - **A skill whose words are all names of languages is dropped** ("English", "Deutsch", "español"): a language is listed apart, with the rules of the languages (#354). A skill that holds such a name among other words ("business English") stays.
    - **A clause of leisure** holds a word of leisure from a short closed list per language ("free time", "hobby", "like"; "tempo libero", "piacciono"; "Freizeit", "mag"; "tiempo libre", "gustan"; "temps libre", "aime"; "tempo livre", "gosto"), and otherwise only the quotes of the draft's interests and function or time words: "In my free time I like chess". A fragment of interests that a comma or a conjunction splits off from such a clause is one too: "and hiking".
    - **Any other mention by the person keeps the skill.** "I am proficient in Python", a job's clause ("where I do portrait photography") or a list of skills ("My skills include chess") claim the ability, so the skill stays wherever else the person mentions it.
    - **Only the clauses that claim count,** as rule (c) reads them: another person's clause ("My sister teaches chess") and a clause of the person that denies, wishes or supposes ("I don't play chess") neither keep the skill nor drop it. When no clause that claims holds the skill whole, nothing tells, and the skill stays.
    - **An interest the description does not give removes no skill:** only the interests that grounding keeps count.
  - **A leading indefinite article** ("a", "una", "eine", "une", "um"…) is trimmed from an employer or a school when it is written in lower case. What is left is still a quote, and a name such as "UNA Hotels" keeps its article.
  - **A degree made only of words of graduating, generic degree words and function words is dropped** ("diplôme", "Abschluss", "laurea", "my degree"). The school stays, and a degree with its field stays.
  - **The use case lists the jobs:** the current job first, then the others by their latest year, newest first. Jobs without a year, and jobs of the same year, keep the model's order.

## Consequences

- **Thinner drafts.** A clause that holds descriptive words the user did not tie to a job, such as "a busy hospital", backs no facts.
- **Little prose.** The model paraphrases, so most of its summary and role sentences are dropped. What stays is the user's own wording. The draft's value is in its structure: entries, dates, skills, languages and quoted achievements.
- **Accepted readings of a claim (c).** Two readings of the claim rule are accepted as they are:
  - **A negation that governs a high level denies the level only.** So "I have never been fluent in German" keeps German, without a level, as "I am not an expert in Swift" keeps Swift.
  - **A condition at the start of a clause makes the rest of its sentence a supposition**, habits included: "If I have time, I play chess" and "Wenn ich nicht arbeite, spiele ich Schach" give no chess.
- **Accepted losses (c).** Losing beats inventing (f):
  - "could" and German "konnte", which folds with "könnte", withdraw a claim, so "I could already read Latin at school" gives no Latin.
  - A negation of the verb withdraws the item though it only denies a level: "I don't speak French fluently" gives no French.
  - Denying a low level reads as no knowledge, though it can mean more: "I'm not a beginner in Python" gives no Python.
  - "'d" before an irregular past that the English verb lists do not hold, or before an adverb, reads as "would": "I'd spent years with Swift" and "I'd previously used Swift" give no Swift. "hätte" alone is never a fact either: "Ich hätte die Stelle fast bekommen" gives nothing. Portuguese "séria" and Spanish "seria" (serious) fold to the Portuguese "seria" (would be), so their clause claims nothing: "Soy una persona seria y hablo alemán" gives no German.
  - "gern", "gerne" or "lieber" with a past "wurde" reads as a wish: "Gerne wurde ich in Python geschult" gives no Python.
  - A question the person answers loses its item: "Do I speak French? Yes, fluently." gives no French. A skill between quotation marks goes too: "I work with “Swift” every day" gives no Swift.
  - A quote that a single mark closes right after a word takes the rest of its line, as the mark may be an apostrophe: "I use 'clean code' principles with Swift every day" gives no Swift, and "My colleague said 'I speak French'. I speak German." no German. So does an apostrophe that starts a word, when a later mark may close it: in "I grew up in the '90s.", then "I built my clients' apps with Swift." on the next line, both lines claim nothing, and Swift goes. A quote that only such marks may close runs to the line of the last of them, though that mark closes another quote: "I use 'clean code'.", "I speak French." and "I use 'unit tests'." on three lines give no French.
  - An infinitive after a relative claims nothing outside the periphrasis of obligation: "En mi trabajo hay que hablar inglés", "Tengo mucho que aprender de Python" and "Ho trovato un team con cui parlare inglese ogni giorno" give no English and no Python.
  - A relative reads as another subject though its subject is the person, or a thing of the person's work: "I am a developer who knows Swift", "Sono una persona che ama la fotografia", "I build apps that use Swift", "Sviluppo app che usano Swift" and "Lo que más me gusta es el ajedrez" give no Swift, photography or chess. Telling a person from a thing would take a list of nouns, which no closed list can hold.
  - A line with no verb before a later wish in its sentence shares the wish: "Fluent in English, and I'd love to learn German" gives no English, "Skills: Python and SQL, but I would like to learn Rust" no Python and no SQL, "Teacher at Acme since 2016, and I would like to move to Berlin" no headline title, and "Languages: Italian, English and French, which I'd like to improve" no language.
- **A residual risk in four languages.** The device tags no verbs in Italian, Spanish, French or Portuguese. So a wish stated after the name, with no infinitive and no factual verb ("Presso Acme sogno dal 2027"), can keep an entry. The entry holds only the quoted name: that clause backs no date, figure or achievement. Reading words after a verbless name as unexplained would drop real certifications and degrees ("ACLS certifications from the Italian Resuscitation Council"), so the rule stops at the lead-in. The editor's banner asks the user to check the draft.
- **A residual risk of the rules: text typed without accents.** The word rules read the moods of supposition by their written form, and a description typed without accents or umlauts can lose the mark that tells a wish from a fact. "Am liebsten wurde ich Spanisch lernen" (for "würde") keeps Spanisch. The contract holds it as a known issue of the rules. The claim check of [ADR 0028](0028-check-each-personal-claim-of-the-ai-draft-again.md), which runs after them, withdraws it.
- **Residual risks of the rules: whose words, whose title and place, whose verb.** Four families need a reading of the relations inside a clause, which the word rules do not build, and the contract holds one row of each per language where it leaks, or one in all, as known issues of the rules. The claim check of ADR 0028 closes all four on the product path.
  - A claim gives the person the title and the place it holds, whoever they are: "Io lavoro con un infermiere a Torino", "I work with a nurse in Turin" and their translations give the person the nurse's title.
  - Another person's words that follow the colon on the colon's own line, without quotation marks, are read by their own clauses: "My colleague wrote: I speak French. I speak German." keeps German. Only a line break tells where such words begin.
  - A noun subject before a relative that keeps the person gives the person the noun's verb after the relative: "The team that I lead uses Kotlin", "Il team che dirigo usa Kotlin" and their translations give the person Kotlin.
  - The relatives of place ("where", "dove", "où", "donde", "onde") are not in the relative list, so the subject they bring in is read as the person: "Trabajo en una empresa donde todo el mundo habla inglés" and its translations give the person the language. German writes a comma before "wo", and the clause it splits off has a subject of its own, so "Ich arbeite in einer Firma, wo alle Französisch sprechen" gives no French.
- **A residual risk: a mark alone on its line in a message that no mark closes.** A lone mark closes the quote that is open unless a later mark closes it and no quote opened after the lone one takes that mark (c). A message that holds a lone symbol, such as a ditto mark, and has no closing mark of its own reads as closed at the symbol, so the lines after it claim as usual: nothing in the text says that the message goes on. The claim check of ADR 0028 does not tell them apart either: it read "I speak French." after a ditto mark as the person's with every instruction it measured but one.
- **Drafts wait for the language data.** On a device that has not yet downloaded the data of English or German, a description in either waits up to 60 seconds for the system to give it, and gives no draft when it does not come: the person then writes the CV another way. The screen says that it waits for the data, in place of the model's "up to a minute", which does not hold for this wait.
- **Residual risks of the contacts.** Two constructions still keep a third party's contact (b):
  - a lowercase name in a fragment after the person's clause, as only capitals mark a name there: "I'm Maria Rossi, a friend of anna verdi. Email: anna@example.com";
  - a capitalised name that equals a quote of the draft elsewhere, such as an employer, as a quote of the draft counts as explained: "I worked at Anna Verdi. I'm Maria Rossi, a friend of Anna Verdi. Email: anna@example.com".

  They are accepted: neither construction is likely in a person's own description, and the draft opens unsaved in the editor with the AI-draft notice, so the person reviews it before saving. The contract pins both as residual risks, which fail once a later rule closes them.
- **Deterministic.** The behaviour is deterministic and stated as a table of examples. Every past defect is a permanent row.
- **Less code.** The recognisers for employer prepositions, capitalised names, location prepositions and temporal lead-ins are removed. Capitals are read in one place only: a fragment that a verbless contact leans on (b). The rules shrink to quotes, clauses and one vocabulary list per language.
- **Upkeep.** The vocabulary lists need care in six languages. A missing word only drops data; it never lets data through.

## Alternatives considered

- **Keep recognising employers and names in free text.** Rejected: nine rounds did not converge.
- **Ship version 1 without the draft.** Rejected by the owner.
- **No prose at all,** only quoted fields. Rejected: prose quoted from the user's own clauses keeps the user's claims at no extra risk.
- **Prose rebuilt from the description's words** (the first form of rule (d)). Abandoned after review: free function words and omitted words let a sentence reverse who did what, or drop its subject.

## Validation

- **Tests.** `GroundingContractTests` pass, together with the sanitiser's other tests, on the Mac mini.
- **Existing sanitiser tests.** Those that this ADR reverses are rewritten in the commit that implements it, each one named in the commit. Any safety property they pinned exists as a contract row first.
- **The real model.** Each run is recorded with its kept and dropped fields. On 2026-10-18 Apple's on-device model on macOS 27.0.1 drafted the six in-app example descriptions and one realistic description, three times each:
  - **Nothing was invented** in any of the 21 runs.
  - **Every employer** (12 of 12) and **every job title** (12 of 12) of the examples were kept, after the fixes of rules (c) and (g).
  - **Each job's own years** were kept only where the model wrote them: in English and Portuguese. In German, Spanish, French and Italian the model leaves the years out. That loss is the model's, not the grounding's.
  - **On the realistic description**, the current job loses its dates, because its clause holds words no list explains ("cardiology department"). This is the thinner draft of the Consequences.
